Research philosophy

We research markets systematically.

We build and study systematic trading strategies using mathematics, statistics, data, and software. A strategy is a hypothesis that has to survive data, costs, constraints, and out-of-sample evidence. We publish the research and infrastructure openly.

  1. 01 — Data {xt}

    Clean data first

    Collect, clean, and validate financial data before any result built on it is trusted.

  2. 02 — Hypotheses H0

    Testable ideas

    Formulate hypotheses about market behaviour that can be measured and could be wrong.

  3. 03 — Validation σ

    Evidence, not anecdotes

    Backtest under realistic assumptions, then try hard to break the result.

  4. 04 — Implementation ∫ dt

    Risk and costs, explicitly

    Model risk, transaction costs, and execution before a strategy is considered usable.

How we research

From hypothesis to strategy.

A quant does not look at charts and trade. Quantitative trading is a research process: each stage can reject an idea, and most ideas are rejected.

The stages below are the same whether the subject is a momentum signal, a market-making model, or an execution algorithm.

  1. 01

    Hypothesis

    Define a market behaviour or statistical relationship worth investigating, and what evidence would contradict it.

    What do we expect, and why?
  2. 02

    Data

    Collect, clean, normalise, and validate the relevant datasets, using only information that was available at each point in time.

    Is every input correct and point-in-time?
  3. 03

    Signal

    Translate the hypothesis into a measurable quantitative signal with a precise, written definition.

    Can the idea be measured?
  4. 04

    Backtest

    Evaluate the strategy against historical data while accounting for realistic assumptions about costs and timing.

    What would have happened, after costs?
  5. 05

    Validation

    Test robustness across different periods, assets, parameters, and market regimes — and correct for the number of variants tried.

    Does it hold elsewhere and later?
  6. 06

    Risk

    Model exposure, volatility, drawdowns, correlation, liquidity, and position sizing.

    How could this lose money?
  7. 07

    Execution

    Consider transaction costs, slippage, liquidity, and execution constraints before any capital is committed.

    Can it be traded at the assumed cost?
  8. 08

    Evaluation

    Continuously evaluate whether the underlying hypothesis remains supported by evidence — and retire it when it does not.

    Is the evidence still supportive?

Cross-Sectional Momentum Under Implementation Stress

Does a classical momentum signal stay meaningful when reasonable implementation choices change? We tested 12-1 momentum on the 49 US industry portfolios of the Kenneth R. French Data Library, 1927–2026, with the specification, 20 variations, the statistical tests and a decision rule fixed in writing before any result was computed.

Pre-registered verdict: partially robust. The gross effect is robust. Net of trading costs it is fragile: it is not significant after correcting for multiple testing, and it disappears with shorter lookbacks and higher costs.

Strategy library

Strategy Research

Thirteen classes of quantitative strategy, each described the same way: what it tries to capture, why it might work, how it is tested, and where it breaks. Descriptions are educational. None is presented as a strategy that reliably makes money.

Relative value

Statistical Arbitrage

Trades temporary deviations in the relative prices of statistically related securities.

01 · What it captures
Short-lived mispricings between securities whose prices are statistically linked.
02 · Hypothesis
Relationships driven by common factors tend to reassert themselves after temporary dislocations caused by liquidity demand.
03 · Data
Clean, synchronised prices for a broad universe; factor exposures; borrow availability and fees.
04 · Signal
Residual returns after removing common factor exposure, traded when a residual is large relative to its own volatility.
05 · Positions
Many small market- and factor-neutral positions, sized by signal strength and expected holding period.
06 · Risks
Crowding and simultaneous unwinds; relationships that break permanently; short-squeeze and borrow risk.
07 · How to test it
Estimate the residual model out of sample; include realistic costs and borrow fees; analyse capacity.
08 · Limitations
The expected edge per trade is small, so results are highly sensitive to cost and execution assumptions.
Time series · short horizon

Mean Reversion

Positions for the partial reversal of short-term price moves that overshoot.

01 · What it captures
The tendency of some extreme short-term moves to partially reverse.
02 · Hypothesis
Liquidity demand and overreaction push prices temporarily away from fair value.
03 · Data
Daily or intraday prices, volume, and bid-ask spreads.
04 · Signal
Standardised deviation from a moving average, or ranks of recent returns (short-term reversal).
05 · Positions
Contrarian positions with short holding periods, often hedged against the market.
06 · Risks
Moves driven by news that do not revert; trends that continue; high turnover.
07 · How to test it
Separate the prices used for signals from those used for execution, to avoid bid-ask bounce; measure net of costs; analyse by regime.
08 · Limitations
Apparent reversal in closing-price data can be an artefact of microstructure noise rather than a tradable effect.
Cross-sectional

Momentum

Ranks assets by past performance and favours recent relative winners over recent relative losers.

01 · What it captures
The tendency of relative winners to keep outperforming relative losers over intermediate horizons.
02 · Hypothesis
Investors under-react to new information and then herd, so relative performance persists for months.
03 · Data
Total returns for a defined universe — stocks, industries, or asset classes — free of survivorship bias.
04 · Signal
Past 12-month return skipping the most recent month (the “12-1” signal), ranked across the universe.
05 · Positions
Long the top bucket and short the bottom, rebalanced periodically; equal-, value- or volatility-weighted.
06 · Risks
Sharp “momentum crashes” when markets rebound after declines; high turnover; crowding.
07 · How to test it
Vary lookback, holding period, universe, and costs; examine subperiods and market states; correct for multiple testing.
08 · Limitations
Much of the classical evidence overlaps the data on which the effect was first documented.

Time series · multi-asset

Trend Following

Follows the direction of each market's own price trend, typically across many futures markets.

01 · What it captures
Sustained directional moves in individual markets.
02 · Hypothesis
Gradual diffusion of information and risk-transfer flows create persistent trends.
03 · Data
Continuous futures prices with careful roll adjustment, contract specifications, and volatility estimates.
04 · Signal
The sign or strength of past returns, or moving-average crossovers, over several horizons.
05 · Positions
Long rising markets and short falling ones, scaled so each market contributes a similar level of risk.
06 · Risks
Whipsaws in range-bound markets, sharp reversals, and long losing streaks.
07 · How to test it
Multi-decade, multi-asset histories; checks on roll methodology; sensitivity across lookbacks.
08 · Limitations
Results arrive in infrequent episodes, so short samples say very little.
Cross-sectional

Factor Investing

Systematic exposure to characteristics associated with differences in average returns.

01 · What it captures
Premia linked to characteristics such as value, size, quality, profitability, or low volatility.
02 · Hypothesis
Premia reflect compensation for risk, persistent behavioural biases, or structural constraints on investors.
03 · Data
Point-in-time fundamentals, prices, market capitalisation, and corporate actions.
04 · Signal
Characteristic scores such as book-to-price, standardised within sectors or regions.
05 · Positions
Long-short factor portfolios or tilted long-only portfolios, often combining several factors.
06 · Risks
Long periods of underperformance, crowding, and unintended sector or macroeconomic exposures.
07 · How to test it
Point-in-time data only; out-of-sample periods and regions; controls for known factors.
08 · Limitations
Many published factors fail to replicate; data mining is a central concern.
Relative value

Pairs Trading

Trades the spread between two closely related securities.

01 · What it captures
Reversion in the spread between two securities with a stable long-run relationship.
02 · Hypothesis
Economically linked securities are cointegrated: the spread between them tends to revert to an equilibrium.
03 · Data
Synchronised prices, corporate actions, and borrow availability.
04 · Signal
The z-score of the spread relative to its estimated equilibrium, with entry and exit thresholds.
05 · Positions
Long the relatively cheap leg, short the relatively expensive leg, with a hedge ratio from the estimated relationship.
06 · Risks
Structural breaks — mergers, regulation, business changes — that permanently move the spread.
07 · How to test it
Select pairs and estimate parameters out of sample, and account for the number of pairs screened.
08 · Limitations
Screening thousands of candidate pairs produces spurious relationships by chance.
General framework

Cross-Sectional Strategies

Compares assets with each other at a point in time, rather than each asset with its own past.

01 · What it captures
Relative differences across a universe, while staying neutral to the overall market.
02 · Hypothesis
Some characteristics predict which assets will do better or worse than their peers.
03 · Data
Comparable features across assets: returns, fundamentals, analyst estimates, liquidity.
04 · Signal
Ranks or z-scores of one or more characteristics, often neutralised to industries and risk factors.
05 · Positions
Long high-ranked and short low-ranked assets, with constraints on exposures and turnover.
06 · Risks
Hidden common exposures, crowding, and universe-construction choices that drive results.
07 · How to test it
Information coefficients, bucket spreads, signal decay, and robustness to the universe definition.
08 · Limitations
A strategy can be market-neutral on average and still carry material risk in stress periods.
Derivatives

Volatility Strategies

Trades the level, shape, or dynamics of volatility.

01 · What it captures
Differences between implied and subsequently realised volatility, or structure in the volatility surface.
02 · Hypothesis
Option prices embed a volatility risk premium and term-structure patterns that are partly predictable.
03 · Data
Option prices and implied volatility surfaces, realised volatility from high-frequency data, volatility futures.
04 · Signal
Implied minus forecast realised volatility; term-structure slope; skew measures.
05 · Positions
Delta-hedged option positions or volatility futures, sized to explicit stress-loss limits.
06 · Risks
Severe losses in volatility spikes, gap risk, and liquidity that disappears when it is most needed.
07 · How to test it
Realistic option spreads and hedging costs, with every stress period included.
08 · Limitations
Strategies that sell volatility can look smooth for years before a single large loss.
Microstructure

Market Making

Provides liquidity by quoting both sides of a market.

01 · What it captures
The bid-ask spread, net of losses to better-informed traders.
02 · Hypothesis
With good quote and inventory management, spread income exceeds adverse-selection losses.
03 · Data
High-frequency order-book, trade and quote data; latency measurements; fee schedules.
04 · Signal
Fair-value estimates, order-flow imbalance, and short-term volatility, used to set and skew quotes.
05 · Positions
Continuous two-sided quotes, skewed to manage inventory, within strict position limits.
06 · Risks
Adverse selection, inventory risk in fast markets, and technology or latency failures.
07 · How to test it
Order-book replay with queue-position-aware fill modelling.
08 · Limitations
Simple backtests overstate fills; realistic order-book simulation is difficult.
Allocation

Portfolio Optimisation

Combines signals and assets into one portfolio under risk and cost constraints.

01 · What it captures
A better balance of expected return, risk, and trading cost for a given set of forecasts.
02 · Hypothesis
Modelling risk and costs explicitly improves the risk-adjusted outcome of the same signals.
03 · Data
Return forecasts, covariance estimates, transaction-cost models, and constraints.
04 · Signal
Not a signal itself: it takes signals as inputs and produces target weights.
05 · Positions
Mean-variance with shrinkage, risk parity, or robust methods, with turnover penalties.
06 · Risks
Estimation error amplified by the optimiser; concentration in a few positions.
07 · How to test it
Out-of-sample comparison against simple baselines such as equal or inverse-volatility weights.
08 · Limitations
Sophisticated optimisers often fail to beat naive allocation once estimation error is included.
Event studies

Event-Driven Quantitative Research

Systematically studies price behaviour around defined events.

01 · What it captures
Repeatable patterns around events such as earnings announcements, index changes, or corporate actions.
02 · Hypothesis
Markets process some kinds of information gradually, or face predictable flows around events.
03 · Data
Precisely timestamped event data, prices, estimates, and index-rebalance schedules.
04 · Signal
Measures of surprise, abnormal returns, and post-announcement drift.
05 · Positions
Positions opened around events with defined holding windows, hedged against market and sector.
06 · Risks
Timing errors, crowding around well-known events, and small samples per event type.
07 · How to test it
Event studies with correct timestamps — when information was public, not when it was recorded — and proper controls.
08 · Limitations
Revised or back-filled event data easily introduces look-ahead bias.
Statistical learning

Machine Learning

Applies statistical learning to forecasting and to combining signals.

01 · What it captures
Nonlinear or interacting relationships that simpler models miss.
02 · Hypothesis
Some predictive structure in financial data is too complex for linear models but stable enough to learn.
03 · Data
Large feature sets — prices, fundamentals, alternative data — built strictly point-in-time.
04 · Signal
Model forecasts from methods such as regularised regression, gradient-boosted trees, or neural networks.
05 · Positions
Forecasts feed portfolio construction like any other signal.
06 · Risks
Overfitting, data leakage, instability when regimes change, and limited interpretability.
07 · How to test it
Purged and embargoed cross-validation, walk-forward evaluation, and comparison with simple baselines.
08 · Limitations
Financial data has a low signal-to-noise ratio, so flexible models fit noise easily.
Implementation

Execution Algorithms

Decides how to trade a target position — not what to trade.

01 · What it captures
A lower total cost of reaching a target position: spread, market impact, and delay.
02 · Hypothesis
Schedules and order types that adapt to liquidity reduce implementation shortfall.
03 · Data
Order-book and trade data, historical fills, intraday volume profiles, and impact estimates.
04 · Signal
Short-term liquidity and price forecasts used to time child orders.
05 · Positions
Schedules such as VWAP, TWAP, or implementation-shortfall algorithms, with participation limits.
06 · Risks
Information leakage, underestimated impact, and adverse price moves during long schedules.
07 · How to test it
Transaction-cost analysis against the arrival price, ideally with controlled comparisons.
08 · Limitations
Market impact is hard to measure: the price path without your own trade is never observed.

Validation

Backtesting is the beginning, not the conclusion.

A backtest shows what a rule would have done under stated assumptions. It does not show that the rule will work in live markets. The job of validation is to find out how easily a good-looking result falls apart.

Test design

In-sample vs. out-of-sample
Fit on one period; judge on another the model has never seen.
Walk-forward analysis
Re-estimate on a rolling window and test on the period that follows, repeatedly.
Parameter sensitivity
A result that works for only one parameter value is probably noise.
Regime changes
Check behaviour across rate cycles, crises, and high- and low-volatility periods.

Biases

Look-ahead bias
Using information that was not available at the time of the decision.
Survivorship bias
Testing only on assets that still exist today and ignoring those that failed.
Data leakage
Test-period information seeping into model fitting through features or preprocessing.
Overfitting
Fitting noise. The more variants tried, the higher the bar for any single result.

Frictions

Transaction costs
Commissions, spreads, and fees — often enough to erase a gross effect.
Slippage
The gap between the price a backtest assumes and the price actually obtained.
Liquidity constraints
Positions must be tradable in size without moving the price against you.
Historical simulation

What a rule would have done

Computed from historical data under stated assumptions about costs, timing, and execution. Useful for rejecting ideas; limited as evidence for accepting them.

Live trading performance

What actually happened

Results of real orders, with real costs and constraints. ScaleX labels every published result as one or the other. Everything published so far is historical simulation.

Risk management

A strategy is only as useful as its risk model.

A return forecast says what might be earned. A risk model says what could be lost, how quickly, and in which situations. We treat it as the part of a strategy that decides whether it can be used at all.

Position sizing
How much capital each position receives, given its risk and conviction.
Volatility
Expected variability of returns; the usual basis for scaling positions.
Correlation
How positions move together — especially in stress, when correlations tend to rise.
Concentration
Exposure to a single asset, sector, factor, or idea.
Drawdown
Peak-to-trough loss, and how long recovery takes.
Liquidity
Whether positions can be reduced quickly without large costs.
Leverage
Borrowed exposure that magnifies both gains and losses.
Tail risk
Rare, extreme outcomes that normal-distribution models understate.
Model risk
The possibility that the model is wrong, or no longer applies.
Execution risk
Losses from failed, delayed, or badly priced trades.

Market data

Data is the raw material.

A sophisticated model cannot compensate for flawed data.

Most backtest errors begin in the data: a missing delisting, a restated earnings figure, a timestamp in the wrong time zone. In our published research, data handling is documented and the checks — including tests for look-ahead errors — are part of the code.

Infrastructure

Research needs infrastructure.

Quantitative research depends on more than mathematical models. Results are only as trustworthy as the systems that produce them: data pipelines, research environments, backtesting engines, risk checks, and monitoring.

This is the reference architecture our research is organised around. Components are documented and released through Ctrl + C when they are published.

  1. DataL1

    1. 01Market DataPrices, volume, fundamentals, order books, alternative data.
    2. 02Data PipelineCleaning, adjustment, point-in-time versioning, validation.

  2. ResearchL2

    1. 03Research EnvironmentPinned dependencies, recorded configs, reproducible runs.
    2. 04Signal GenerationDocumented signal definitions, computed point-in-time.
    3. 05BacktestingCosts, timing, and constraints modelled explicitly.

  3. PortfolioL3

    1. 06Portfolio ConstructionCombines signals into target weights under constraints.
    2. 07Risk EngineExposure, limits, and stress tests before any order.

  4. ProductionL4

    1. 08ExecutionOrder scheduling, cost control, fill reconciliation.

Monitoring feeds back into research. When live behaviour diverges from the model, the hypothesis is re-examined.

About ScaleX

Why open quantitative finance?

Financial research has traditionally been difficult to inspect from the outside. We believe many of the tools, methods, and ideas used in quantitative finance can benefit from the same principles that transformed software engineering: reproducibility, collaboration, transparency, and rigorous testing.

ScaleX is building quantitative finance in the open. We research systematic strategies the way an engineering team builds software: with versioned code, written plans, automated tests, and a public record of what did not work.

Openness raises the standard. A result that anyone can rerun has to be careful about data, costs, and statistics — and when it is wrong, someone can find out.
